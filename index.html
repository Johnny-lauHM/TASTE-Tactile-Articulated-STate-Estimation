TASTE · Tactile Articulated STate Estimation

Solve a Rubik’s cube
by touch.

Two dexterous hands estimate the cube’s pose and its turning layer from fingertip touch and joint angles alone, and solve it with no camera in the loop.

Simulation (Isaac Lab). Two Sharpa Wave SE hands on Franka FR3 arms. The controllers see only the touch-based estimate; the simulator’s state is used for scoring.

How it works

Feel. Estimate. Act on the estimate.

  1. Sense Touch and proprioception

    Finger joint angles and velocities, fingertip force and torque, and a 30×30 tactile image per fingertip pad (TacMap model of the Sharpa Wave SE), plus the skill the robot is executing.

  2. Estimate TASTE estimator

    A small CNN shared across the ten pads feeds a recurrent network (GRU, 384 units). It outputs the cube’s position, orientation and layer angle with their rates, and the probability that the current skill is complete.

  3. Act Skill controllers

    Reinforcement-learned skills (PPO, trained with privileged state) and the regrasp servo receive the estimate in place of the simulator’s state. The completion head triggers the next skill.

  4. Plan Solve as skills

    A cube solver’s face turns are compiled into the hands’ skill alphabet: U/D flicks, R/L wrist rolls, whole-cube x turns and regrasps, choosing the sequence that is most reliable.

Estimator in the loop. The estimator is trained on rollouts driven by its own estimates (DAgger-style: control on the true state is annealed to zero), so it learns from the states its own errors create.

Toward the real hand

Calibrated against the real Wave SE.

Measured latency

Step responses on the real Sharpa Wave SE show about 20 ms from command to motion. With that fixed latency added to the simulated fingers, the sighted system still solves 85.5% (nominal 90.6%), but the blind system drops to 61.3% (nominal 83.2%). Touch-based estimation is the latency-sensitive part.

Tactile delivery

The real sensor refreshes at about 30 Hz. In an earlier system, delivering tactile data 50 ms and 100 ms late lowered blind solves under randomization from 43% to 36% and 25% (128 scrambles each). Models of the real delivery, a camera-measurement baseline and estimators trained on deployment-like states are being evaluated.

Limitations

What this is not, yet.

  • Simulation only. No result on the real robot yet.
  • An idealized cube. Layers snap into line when the grip changes faces. A cube with a separately jointed middle layer, or layers that jam when left out of line, cuts the solve rate sharply.
  • Under audit. We found that the blind system’s cube orientation is re-anchored to the simulator’s value at each skill start. The blind numbers above may therefore be optimistic; the corrected evaluation is pending.
  • Latency. Finger latency spread over 12–42 ms breaks the current skills; real-hand measurements at the raised speed limit will set the training range.
